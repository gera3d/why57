Use your owned social history to reveal the content strategy you are already running.

Recipe at a glance

Daily job
Decide what to keep, change, test, retire, update, or leave alone before content planning turns into more posts.
Inputs
One authorized export, 10–30 public post URLs, or pasted posts with dates, format, and owner-approved metrics.
Output
A Content Pattern Map with evidence, observed signals, strategy hypotheses, gaps, and decisions.
Handoff
The owner verifies metrics and context, chooses the strategy, and approves every future post.
Stop condition
Unclear goal, weak evidence, private data, or invented results means no calendar and no draft.

Three practical starting points

Use the map when a content decision keeps getting rebuilt from memory.

These are illustrative situations. The map makes the evidence and the owner’s check visible before a content plan starts.

Situation 01

LinkedIn teaching posts explain the problem but rarely identify the offer.

Bounded input
12 owner-selected LinkedIn posts from one quarter, plus one sentence naming the current offer.
Useful output
A map of repeated operational-friction stories, the audience language used, and the missing offer or CTA coverage.
Owner check
Confirm the intended audience and current offer before approving one CTA experiment.
Situation 02

A Facebook launch sequence may be a repeatable pattern, or it may be a one-off.

Bounded input
10–15 selected Facebook posts from one launch, with only the metrics the owner is willing to verify.
Useful output
A pattern map that separates observed format and topic repetition from a hypothesis about the launch job.
Owner check
Decide which posts still support the offer and whether one dated test is worth running.
Situation 03

One high-reaction post is starting to pull the next calendar off course.

Bounded input
10 selected posts, including the high-reaction post, and owner-verified metrics only if available.
Useful output
An evidence row that keeps the reaction as an observed signal and leaves any business outcome unproven unless the owner can verify it.
Owner check
Choose whether to test the idea, update it, or leave it alone instead of copying a one-off.
Why save this as a skill

Save the method once. Bring the changing business context each time.

The reusable part is the evidence discipline. The changing part is the business decision. That keeps a later review from reopening the same argument about inputs, metrics, owner approval, and who publishes.

Reusable method
The three safe input paths, the Post Evidence Table, observed-versus-inference labels, red-flag pass, map columns, and approval gate.
Changes per job
The platform, current goal, offer, audience, selected posts, verified metrics, constraints, and dated experiment.
Practical payoff
Reduce re-explaining the workflow and keep private data, invented metrics, and publishing authority outside the task. It does not promise fixed token savings or automatic publishing.

You are helping me create a reviewable Content Pattern Map from my own published social history.

Start by asking me exactly two questions, then stop for my answer:
1. Choose one platform: LinkedIn or Facebook.
2. Choose one current business goal: [offer awareness / qualified conversations / customer education / recruiting / another specific goal].

Then ask me to choose one authorized input:
A. an authorized LinkedIn or Facebook data export, limited to my published-post material;
B. 10–30 public post URLs from the platform I chose; or
C. pasted post text with date, format, and any metrics I am willing to share.

Never require credentials, a full account connection, private messages, contact lists, other creators' content, or an account session. Do not search beyond the URLs or material I supply. Treat missing metrics as unknown. Do not invent engagement, leads, revenue, audience intent, or business outcomes.

First, create a normalized Post Evidence Table. For every post, include:
post label or URL | date | platform | topic | audience | format | hook | point of view | CTA | owner-provided metrics | observed signal | inference / uncertainty.

Then create a Content Pattern Map with these sections:
1. Observable content patterns: repeated topics, audience language, formats, hooks, points of view, CTAs, and cadence. Cite the supporting posts for each pattern.
2. Strategy hypotheses: what each repeated pattern likely tried to achieve. Keep this distinct from observed facts and explain the evidence.
3. Gaps: topic, audience, format, CTA, and offer coverage that is missing or thin against my stated business goal. Mark each gap observed, inferred, or unknown.
4. Decisions: recommend keep, change, test, retire, update, or leave alone for each important pattern or post. Include evidence and confidence: high, medium, or low.
5. One dated experiment worth considering next: name the test date, audience, topic, format, CTA, business-goal link, baseline only if I verified one, and review date. Call it a proposal, never an automatic posting instruction.

Use a clear table with these columns for every meaningful pattern:
pattern | evidence | observed signal | strategy hypothesis | likely job | confidence | keep/change/test/retire | owner question.

Run a content-strategy red-flag pass. Flag and explain:
- treating likes as revenue or proof of demand;
- inferring strategy from one viral post;
- copying another creator's voice;
- generic “post more” advice;
- inventing audience intent;
- optimizing for reach without a business goal; and
- turning research insight into an automatic posting plan.

Stop after the map and ask me to approve, change, test, retire, update, leave alone, or reject each important finding. Do not create a strategy, content calendar, or draft before I approve that next stage.

Only after I approve, ask for my current business goal, offer, audience, constraint, and verified performance data. Then you may propose a strategy or draft for my review. I choose the strategy, approve each post, and personally publish or schedule it.

The actual job

Most founders already have a content strategy. It is just buried in a pile of posts.

Open a social profile and you usually find repeated subjects, familiar formats, recurring asks, and gaps that have started to feel normal. The problem is not a lack of posting ideas. It is that the pattern is hard to see while you are in the middle of publishing.

A Content Pattern Map pulls the pattern into one review surface. It separates what the posts actually show from what the strategy may have been, then gives the owner a smaller decision: keep the useful pattern, change the weak one, test one specific gap, or retire work that no longer supports the business goal.

LinkedIn documents a data-download path that can request selected data categories, and Facebook documents a device-export path where the owner can select information, a date range, and HTML or JSON.12 That does not make an account export the default. A short, owned list of post URLs or pasted posts is often the cleaner starting point.

Context map. The path to a test or draft remains closed until the owner reviews the evidence.

Prepare the smallest useful history.

Pick one platform first. A LinkedIn post may serve a different audience and business job from a Facebook post. Combining them at the start hides that difference and makes every conclusion softer.

Path A

Authorized export

Supply only the published-post material needed for this review. Exclude messages, contacts, and account data with no role in the mapping decision.

Path B

10–30 public post URLs

Choose a meaningful slice: a launch, a quarter, a topic run, or a year of posts you can still explain. The AI reads only those links.

Path C

Pasted post evidence

Use text, date, format, and metrics you are comfortable sharing. A blank metric field stays blank. It does not become a guess.

Research on personalized writing systems supports selective retrieval of historic user-authored material that is relevant to a defined task.3 A broader personalization benchmark also evaluates retrieving profile items for specific tasks.4 These papers support bounded context as a method. They do not prove that this recipe grows an audience or produces a business result.

  • Private messages, credentials, login sessions, contacts, and platform data that does not help explain a published post.
  • Other creators’ posts, comments, or voice as a template for the owner’s strategy.
  • Likes, reach, or reactions presented as revenue, demand, conversion, or customer intent without owner-verified evidence.
  • Posts the owner cannot recognize, explain, or safely review in the context of the current business goal.

Build the map before you build a calendar.

Name one platform and one business goal.

Give the AI a decision frame. “Get more reach” is a channel wish. “Help qualified operations leaders understand the offer before a sales call” is a business goal the map can test against.

Normalize each post before looking for a pattern.

Capture post, date, platform, topic, audience, format, hook, point of view, CTA, and owner-verified metrics. Keep observed wording separate from inferred intent.

Map repetitions and gaps with citations.

Show which posts support a pattern. Repeated founder stories with no offer CTA is an observation. “The audience wants a workshop” is an inference that needs owner review.

Choose a decision for each meaningful pattern.

Use keep, change, test, retire, update, or leave alone. A decision without evidence is a preference. Keep it labeled that way.

Approve the map before strategy or drafting.

The owner changes the record, chooses the strategy, and decides whether one dated experiment or one draft is worth creating. Publishing stays with the owner.

Synthetic scorecard. “Do not generalize” is a useful decision because it prevents a metric from being turned into a strategy claim.

Run the taste test before a pattern becomes a plan.

A map should make the next choice easier to defend. Score it before you ask for a strategy, calendar, or draft.

Strategic relevanceDoes every pattern connect to the one current business goal, or did the map become a catalogue of posts?
Evidence qualityCan each pattern point back to specific owner-authorized posts, dates, and observable details?
Business-goal fitDoes the proposed decision name an offer, desired action, or decision the business actually cares about?
Audience clarityIs the audience stated in the post or brief, or clearly marked as an owner-checkable inference?
OriginalityDoes the map surface the owner’s own point of view instead of borrowing another creator’s voice or content formula?
PrivacyDid the review stay inside selected published posts and owner-approved metrics, with no private messages or credentials?
Editing burdenCan the owner correct a few rows, or does the central logic need to be rebuilt from scratch?

Stop condition: Rebuild the map if it cannot cite its evidence, treats engagement as a business outcome, invents audience intent, drifts from the goal, or pressures the owner into an automatic posting plan.

Keep the authority line visible.

AI may do

  • Classify owner-authorized published posts and surface observable patterns.
  • Separate direct evidence, owner-verified metrics, uncertainty, and strategy hypotheses.
  • Propose a bounded experiment and draft only after explicit owner approval.
  • Flag weak evidence, privacy issues, and content-strategy red flags.

The owner must do

  • Authorize data and select the platform, post set, and current business goal.
  • Verify metrics, offer context, audience, and any business outcome.
  • Choose the strategy and approve each post or experiment.
  • Personally publish or schedule, or decide to do nothing.

Map one content strategy before you make more content.

Choose the platform where you have enough published history to recognize the context. Pick one business goal. Give the prompt 10–30 posts you can review line by line. Keep the map honest, then choose one dated experiment only if it earns the owner’s approval.
